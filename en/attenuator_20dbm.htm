VARIABLE ATTENUATOR

0-20dBm 100 kHz - 3000 MHz

Radio & antennasRoberto Chiriorubric "Radio Corner"

Premise

December 2016 

What is an attenuator for? to reduce RF voltage value to a lower value, without losing the impedance value of the line coupling always at 50 OHM.

It can happen that the received signal is too high thus saturating the receiver input, so if it is not possible to attenuate directly in the receiver you must reduce the antenna signal through resistive dividers suitable for RF.

It is easy to find fixed and variable attenuators used in TV antenna systems and also for use in electronic equipment.

Attenuators for radio reception use are harder to find and are expensive.

Using a variable attenuator for TV-SAT use like the one in the photo you can get good results in radio reception. Useful to make some modifications to adapt the product to radio reception.

Description

  

This is the diagram of a fixed attenuator with 20dB attenuation value calculated with the RFSIM99 program.

By setting the input and output impedance values and the attenuation value we get the calculation of the resistances for the Pi network configuration.

By inserting the divider on the receiver or equipment input always at 50 Ohm we will get a 20 dB reduction equal to 100 times the input value, that is 1 V becomes 0.01 V equal to 10 mV.

To make a variable attenuator we would need to modify the value of all three resistances, to keep the input output value constant.

All this is easily obtainable with a multi-turn trimmer like the blue one found in the TV-Sat attenuator. It's a multi-turn variable resistor with several brushes inside that slide on carbon tracks; displacement achieves variation at constant impedance. 

 

These are the simple modifications to make on the commercial TV-Sat attenuator: remove the inductor for DC passage soldered between the two connectors and optionally solder two more commonly used connectors in the radio field like BNC or panel-mount SMA connectors.

The attenuator is for 75 ohm line impedance, going to 50 ohm you get practically the same signal reduction results, for reception I would say it's fine as is, but for those who want to use it with test equipment it is good to solder a 150 ohm noninductive resistor on each of the two connectors to ground to adapt input and output to 50 ohm.

By turning the blue trimmer it is possible to obtain the attenuation variation. The attenuator is given for a frequency range of 0.1 - 2400 MHz.

Tested with instrumentation it easily reaches 3000 MHz, and at the low end it behaves well down to 50-100 kHz but it can go lower, the minimum frequency is limited by the two ceramic capacitors present in the metal box, they are 220nF, going overboard you can bring them to 680nF so as to be sure to reach down to 10 kHz without great losses. On the other hand if you already have galvanic isolation of the signal in other points of the line, I would say that the capacitors can also be removed by connecting the blue trimmer directly to the input and output. It has been verified that the trimmer is symmetrical so the input/output connectors are not binding, you can connect straight or inverted, the result in terms of attenuation is always the same.

The reception test was carried out with the MiniWhip antenna and by rotating the blue trimmer, a signal reduction was obtained across the entire receivable range, and indeed the stronger stations were attenuated so as to improve reception and disturb less at the level of intermodulation the weaker receptions.

The attenuator should be mounted behind or near the receiver so as to easily adjust it, moreover it must be downstream of those antennas that require coaxial power.

In practice with a small expense you get a convenient attenuator for reception use and laboratory.
